Cancer Mortality in South Africa

Trends in cancer deaths with a focus on HIV-associated malignancies, 1997–2022

Annual monitoring of cancer mortality from South African vital registration data. Special focus on HIV-associated cancers — cervical cancer, Kaposi’s sarcoma, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma — and the top cancers by burden in the South African population.
Published

March 13, 2026

1 Introduction

South Africa carries an exceptionally high burden of cancer, shaped by three intersecting forces:

  1. The HIV epidemic. South Africa has the largest HIV-positive population in the world (~7.8 million, UNAIDS 2023). Severe immunosuppression dramatically elevates the risk of infection-driven cancers — particularly cervical cancer (HPV), Kaposi’s sarcoma (HHV-8), and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (EBV). The scale-up of antiretroviral therapy (ART) since 2004 has altered incidence trajectories, but cervical cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death in South African women.

  2. The epidemiological transition. Urbanisation, changing diets, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption have driven rising rates of colorectal, breast, lung, and oesophageal cancers — patterns seen across sub-Saharan Africa as it undergoes demographic and economic transition.

  3. Late-stage diagnosis. Limited access to cancer screening, diagnostic pathology services, and treatment facilities means that a high proportion of cancer deaths in South Africa reflect advanced or undiagnosed disease. Vital registration data therefore likely undercount the true burden.

This analysis uses the MRC-processed South African vital registration file (1997–2022) to monitor trends and assess the relative burden of the top cancer groups.

1.1 HIV-associated malignancies

Three cancers are classified as AIDS-defining or strongly HIV-associated:

Cancer ICD-10 HIV mechanism
Cervical cancer C53 HPV — persistent infection, impaired immune clearance
Kaposi’s sarcoma C46 HHV-8 — reactivation under immunosuppression
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma C82–C86, C96 EBV and immune dysregulation

Cervical cancer is the most prevalent of the three and the single most common cancer death in South African women. Its trajectory is critical for evaluating the combined impact of HIV treatment, HPV vaccination, and cervical screening programmes.

2 Data

Cancer deaths are identified from the underlying cause of death field (UnderlyingCause) using ICD-10 three-character prefixes. Only natural, individual deaths with an underlying cause starting with C are included. Stillbirths and unnatural deaths are excluded.

4 Discussion

4.1 HIV–cancer intersection

The most striking feature of cancer mortality in South Africa compared with other middle-income countries is the dominance of infection-driven cancers. Cervical cancer, driven by HPV in the context of HIV-mediated immune failure, accounts for the majority of female cancer deaths in many years. This reflects:

  • High HIV prevalence in women of reproductive age with inadequate viral suppression.
  • Low screening coverage: cytology-based screening has historically covered <30% of eligible women.
  • Late presentation: most women present at FIGO stage III–IV; treatment intent is palliative.

The national HPV vaccination programme (Cervarix, then Gardasil — girls, grade 4, since 2014) will not be visible in VR mortality data until the vaccinated cohort enters peak cervical cancer risk ages (~40–60 years), i.e. approximately 2040+. However, trends in cervical precancer (CIN3) from colposcopy registries may show earlier signal.

4.2 ART roll-out and Kaposi’s sarcoma

Kaposi’s sarcoma (C46) mortality should decline as ART coverage and viral suppression improve. A sustained plateau or rise in KS deaths signals either: - ART access failure or late treatment initiation. - HHV-8 reactivation despite ART in patients with incomplete immune reconstitution.

4.3 Oesophageal cancer

South Africa, particularly the Eastern Cape (former Transkei), has one of the highest oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) incidence rates in the world — estimated 10× global average. Risk factors include maize-based spirits (home-brewed), tobacco, nutritional deficiencies, and possibly HPV (a minority of cases). Monitoring this trend is important as a measure of poverty-linked cancer burden.

4.4 Limitations

  1. Under-certification: cancer as underlying cause may be under-recorded where cancer was diagnosed late or where another acute cause (sepsis, respiratory failure) was certified.
  2. ICD-10 coding quality: the VR data uses the 3-character block for underlying cause; sub-site specificity (e.g. C53.1 endocervical vs C53.0 exocervical) is not analysed here.
  3. Population denominators: counts only — age-standardised rates, incidence–fatality ratios, and comparative analyses require census-based denominators.
  4. No staging or treatment data: VR data cannot distinguish early-stage from late-stage diagnosis.
