Rob’s Request: Adjusted CYP and Recorded Births

Redistributing long-acting contraceptive protection across calendar years and comparing it with recorded births.
Published

September 21, 2026

1 At a glance: objectives, adjustments, outputs

This page tests Rob’s proposed change to the CYP calculation against the original method used in the main indicators report, where each service is credited its full CYP factor in the year it was delivered.

NoteScope and data used on this page

Condoms are excluded from the old-versus-new comparison, because they are 45–72% of all CYP when counted as fully used and their data change grain in 2018 (see the condom sensitivity page). One facility-month implant entry that was about 10,000 times its normal value (Botleng Ext 6 CHC, March 2025) is corrected in wrangling.R and recorded in data_corrections_log. A plain-language walk-through with worked examples is on the CYP walk-through page.

2 Measures compared

The old measure is the standard CYP presented in the main DHIS report. It credits all expected protection from a long-acting method in its insertion year. The new measure spreads implant and IUCD protection across the calendar years in which it is expected to remain active and applies the proposed pill and implant factors.

A bridge measure, timing only, redistributes implant and IUCD protection while preserving every old standard factor, isolating the timing change from the additional factor changes in the full proposal.

Important

This is an exploratory ecological comparison of two national annual series. A correlation does not estimate contraceptive effectiveness or establish that contraception caused the observed birth trend. Both series may be affected by reporting completeness, method mix, population size and unmeasured changes over time.

Recorded births are defined as live births in facilities plus births born alive before arrival. Stillbirths are excluded. The standard CYP series is unchanged from the main report.

3 Allocation assumptions

The adjusted measure applies each year’s method-specific weight to services recorded in year \(N-k\), where \(k\) is the protection-year offset. Thus, adjusted protection in calendar year \(N\) is

\[ \operatorname{Adjusted\ CYP}_N = \sum_m \sum_k \operatorname{Services}_{m,N-k} w_{m,k}. \]

The full proposal uses an implant profile of \(1.00, 0.80, 0.70\), totalling 2.5 CYP. For the timing-only bridge, the same relative profile is scaled to total 3.5 CYP so that only timing changes. The working IUCD profile starts at 0.85 and 0.75 and declines over ten years, totalling 4.6 CYP in both new measures. Pills use 12 cycles per CYP in the full proposal and the old 15-cycle factor in the timing-only bridge. These are scenario assumptions, not locally estimated continuation rates.

Warning

The full old-versus-new difference combines a timing change with two factor changes: pill credit rises by 25% per cycle and total implant credit falls by 28.6% per insertion. It must not be interpreted as the effect of continuation allocation alone. The timing-only bridge provides that separate comparison.

4 How lagged weights build up each year

For a given calendar year, adjusted CYP for a method is the sum of services from the current and earlier years, each multiplied by its decayed weight. 5 Worked example: sub-dermal implants

This example uses implants to show how the yearly figure is accumulated, using the observed-cohorts-only scenario so that every number comes directly from the recorded insertions \(D_s\). Weights are taken from the matrix above: \(w_0 = 1.00\), \(w_1 = 0.80\), \(w_2 = 0.70\). For each calendar year \(t\),

\[ P_t = 1.00\,D_t + 0.80\,D_{t-1} + 0.70\,D_{t-2}. \]

Each row of the table adds the three terms in turn. The “running total” columns show the sum after each lag is added, so the last one equals \(P_t\).

Each insertion contributes 2.5 CYP over its lifetime, but the adjusted method spreads it over three years. Where insertions are rising, adjusted CYP is below standard credit at 2.5 because part of each cohort’s protection falls in later years. Where insertions fall, it is above, because earlier, larger cohorts are still contributing. The first years also omit cohorts inserted before 2015 (see next section). The original standard CYP uses 3.5 per implant and is higher than adjusted CYP in every year shown. The cumulative columns show the gap: adjusted CYP lags the standard credit, and the difference at the end of 2025 is protection from 2024–2025 insertions that falls after the series ends.

7 Pre-2015 cohorts

The DHIS series begins in 2015. Consequently, active implant and IUCD cohorts inserted before 2015 cannot be observed. Two versions of the full proposal show the effect of this boundary:

  • Observed cohorts only: uses only insertions observed from 2015 onward and is therefore left-truncated in early years.
  • Steady-state initialization: assumes the 2015 method-specific count also occurred in each required pre-2015 year. This is a transparent warm-up assumption, not an estimate of historical insertions.

The steady-state version is used for the main old-versus-new comparison. The observed-cohort version is retained only as a boundary sensitivity check.

8 Differences from old standard CYP

The timing-only columns isolate the change from moving protection across calendar years. The full-proposal columns add the changed pill and implant factors.

9 Alignment with recorded births

Two temporal alignments are evaluated with Pearson’s correlation coefficient:

  • Previous-year births: CYP in year \(N\) versus births in year \(N-1\), the relationship motivating this sensitivity analysis.
  • Following-year births: CYP in year \(N\) versus births in year \(N+1\), the hypothesised forecasting relationship.

Each estimate uses ten annual pairs. No p-values are reported because the short, serially ordered time series does not satisfy the independence assumption of a simple correlation test.

10 Correlation with births: old versus new

Births in the following year: old standard CYP \(r = -0.623\), timing only \(r = -0.695\), full proposal \(r = -0.698\).

Births in the previous year: old standard CYP \(r = -0.202\), timing only \(r = -0.271\), full proposal \(r = -0.282\).

12 Converting to a rate (CYPR)

CYP and recorded births above are both national annual counts, so a shared population trend could inflate or deflate their apparent relationship. This section repeats the alignment analysis on a rate scale: CYP and births per 1,000 women of reproductive age (WRA, 15-49), using Stats SA mid-year population estimates (NMCleaner::pop). This is WRA, not married/in-union WRA (MWRA) — the FP2030-standard denominator for CYP rates — because no marital-status population breakdown exists in the source data. The values below should be treated as a documented approximation pending confirmation of the intended denominator.

13 Correlation with births: rate versus raw counts

Births in the following year, rate scale: old standard CYP \(r = -0.816\), timing only \(r = -0.898\), full proposal \(r = -0.902\).

Births in the previous year, rate scale: old standard CYP \(r = -0.589\), timing only \(r = -0.672\), full proposal \(r = -0.689\).

Dividing both series by the female population aged 15–49 removes shared population growth. Compare these rate-scale values with the raw-count values reported earlier: a change between the two scales reflects that shared trend rather than a different relationship. A direct test of whether spreading improves the correlation, by intervention and for counts and rates, is on the CYP walk-through page.

14 Original request

NoteEmail from Rob (kept verbatim for reference)

While the couple year protection rate (CYPR) is highly (negatively) correlated with the numbers of births in the previous calendar year from 2016 to 2023 (i.e. when births are high, women go on contraception (and vice versa) - sort of justifying the trend in births over this period - but this does not hold for the 2024 births (and 2025 CYPR).

Also, ideally, one would prefer to find an association of the CYPR with births in the following year, but this is very weak (mainly because of the way the CYPR is determined - in particular, for example implants are given a weight of 2.5 in the year of implant and then not included in the following years).

So I was wondering if you (because you would be much more efficient at doing it and it would be useful to have the measure available on your GitHub site) could consider computing an Adjusted CYPR where the numerator is computed as described below. Then we could check how well it works, and consider possible refinements.

Structuring the adjusted forecasting table

Contraceptive method Raw distribution (Year N) Standard CYP reporting (credited entirely to Year N) Adjusted allocation (protection timeline)
Oral contraceptive pills 1200 cycles 100 CYP (12 cycles = 1 CYP) 100% credited to Year N (impacts Year N+1 births)
Sub-dermal implant 100 units 250 CYP (credited completely to Year N) Year N: 100 CYP; Year N+1: 80 CYP; Year N+2: 70 CYP
Copper IUD 100 units 460 CYP (credited completely to Year N) Year N: 85 CYP; Year N+1: 75 CYP; …and so on for 10 years

Apply a continuation decay rate (the matrix). Do not just divide a 3-year implant evenly by 3 (0.33 per year). Real-world protection decreases over time because women have implants removed early due to side effects, planning a pregnancy, or reaching menopause. Instead, construct a continuation matrix based on local survey data (e.g. Demographic and Health Surveys) or standard global drop-out assumptions (see the IDS Heart CYP guide and the Data4Impact CYP page).
